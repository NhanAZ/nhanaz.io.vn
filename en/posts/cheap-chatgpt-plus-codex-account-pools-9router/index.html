A $1.50 ChatGPT Plus account does not come from nowhere

Beneath the cheap Codex account market is batch inventory, attrition, warranties, and a routing layer that turns the quota of many accounts into one stable-looking service.

← All writing

An official ChatGPT Plus plan runs on a monthly subscription. A Telegram listing may come with a three-hour warranty. The strangest gap is not the price of roughly $1.50. It is those three hours. If this were merely the same subscription sold at a discount, why would the seller need to end responsibility before the buyer has finished an afternoon of use?

That small detail opens onto a market much larger than the phrase “cheap ChatGPT account.” It has inventory, product codes, batch restocks, stock APIs, wholesale rates, resellers, rented phone numbers, grades for “durable” and “easy to die” accounts, replacement warranties, and gateways that let many customers consume quota without ever seeing the upstream accounts. Codex has made this market hotter because a developer can burn through limits much faster than someone having a few ordinary chats.

This article draws from a Telegram export supplied by its data owner, cross-checked against OpenAI documentation, public source code, security advisories, threat-intelligence research, and community discussions. The goal is to reconstruct a model, not identify people behind aliases or convict a particular individual. I am not publishing credentials, authentication screenshots, session links, bulk-registration recipes, or ways to evade controls.

A three-hour warranty is more honest than the Plus label

In the data I reviewed, products carrying the ChatGPT Plus label ranged from a few tens of thousands to more than one hundred thousand Vietnamese đồng. Cheaper stock usually came with an extremely short warranty or none at all. More expensive stock was described as “durable,” tied to a different mailbox or payment route, or more reliable for Codex. Some shops delivered an email address, password, and two-factor authentication secret. Others sold activation on the customer's account. Another layer sold only a gateway key and endpoint.

“Plus” has therefore stopped functioning as a product definition inside this market. It is a sticker placed on several different access rights. It may be a real but unstable subscription, an account receiving a trial or promotion, a workspace seat, remaining quota, credentials passed through several hands, or merely the ability to send requests through a pool. The customer sees the same logo. The asset behind it may be entirely different.

The buyer thinks they are comparing the monthly price of Plus. The seller may actually be pricing the chance an account survives the next sweep, how many days they will replace it, whether it clears phone verification, how much Codex quota remains, and how much control they have over the upstream. The price sheet sounds like software retail. The operation looks more like selling perishable goods whose expiry date was left off the package.

The dataset cannot describe the whole market, but it reveals a production line

The original export contains 27 chat records, 19 chat folders, 42 images, and a main JSON file of 1,083,207 bytes. The relevant time window runs from July 11 to August 10, 2026. The SHA-256 of the original data file is E50935A1FFE491C8951F6F65791DA4D1495123249F3E56DC6208F9EC3A7BAF9E. A hash does not prove that the content is true, but it helps establish that the analysis refers to the same file rather than a quietly edited replacement.

Eight sources in the export relate directly to ChatGPT or Codex. They show automated menus, QR or wallet payments, restocks of 100 to 500 accounts at a time, self-reported larger batches, very cheap mailboxes, stock-management APIs, referrals, SMS rentals, and credential packaging. A normalized sample from one shop contains 23 ChatGPT stock notices advertising 277 units. Under the extreme assumption that every unit sold at its listed price, theoretical gross revenue would be 16,075,000 VND.

That number is not confirmed revenue and certainly not profit. The export does not contain matching invoices, completed orders, refunds, source costs, or chargebacks. The calculation matters for another reason. Two hundred and seventy-seven listed units in one sample are enough to reject the image of a few people casually selling spare accounts. There is an intake rhythm, grading, distribution, and a process for defective goods.

This is no longer one person sharing an account with a friend. It is a supply chain where defective inventory can fail to log in, run out of quota, or disappear after a few days.

From a four-cent mailbox to an API-shaped endpoint

The most plausible operating chain has five layers. Not every operator owns all five. One person may only retail, another may source stock, another may run the pool, and a website may be nothing more than the storefront. That division of labor is precisely why the final buyer has difficulty knowing where a request really goes.

mailbox, phone, payment route, trial, seat or wholesale source
    → activate and test account health
    → package credentials or import into a pool
    → router selects an account with remaining quota
    → sell an account, slot, key or endpoint
    → replace the source when an account dies

The supply layer has more than one door

The export repeatedly mentions UPI, Apple Pay, GCash, iCloud, Gmail, and Hotmail. These are first of all seller labels for payment routes, mailbox types, and expected lifetimes. OpenAI confirms that UPI is an official payment method for Go and Plus in India. UPI is therefore not the name of a vulnerability. The meaningful questions are the activation conditions, duration of the entitlement, whether resale complies with the terms, and whether one payment source is being scaled far beyond its intended use.

The most likely answer is that several sources coexist. Accounts can be created or activated in batches, bought from upstream wholesalers, receive a trial or promotion, pass through a regional route, originate from seats or workspaces, and then be resold. Compromised credentials and payment fraud may also exist in this market, but the export lacks the chain of custody needed to assign a particular batch to those sources. A low price is a reason to investigate, not a substitute for evidence.

The testing layer turns accounts into graded inventory

Before a sale, an operator needs to know whether an account can log in, what quota remains, which models work, whether phone verification appears, whether the session survives, and whether the customer can change its details. Terms such as “scanned,” “dead,” “durable,” “no warranty,” and “10-day warranty” form a risk-scoring table. They do not necessarily name an attack technique. They describe the probability that the product still works when the customer opens it.

This also explains why stock must be larger than the active customer base. If every batch has substantial attrition, the operator has to overproduce, overbuy, or hold reserve accounts. One LINUX DO thread self-reported testing more than 30 trial accounts through a UPI route and finding only eight alive at the next update. That account cannot be audited, but it fits the export's rhythm of filling stock, sweeps, replacements, and short warranties remarkably well.

The packaging layer decides what the customer can see

Selling credentials is the most direct form. The customer receives an account and signs in. Activation or seats keep the account on the customer's side but send subscription rights through another party. A relay hides the account entirely. The user receives a base URL and key while the operator retains upstream OAuth tokens, sessions, or API keys. These three products do not have the same privacy, ownership, or reliability even when each is advertised with the words ChatGPT or Codex.

The routing layer turns attrition into a service with fewer visible cracks

When a standalone account dies, the customer loses service. In a pool, a dead account may be only a red row on the operator's dashboard. The router lowers the priority of a source nearing its limit, moves the request to another account, refreshes tokens, and leaves the customer-facing endpoint unchanged. Instability does not disappear. It moves from the storefront to behind the curtain.

9Router is an amplifier, not an account factory

This distinction needs precision. The literal string 9Router does not appear in the message text of the export. The data contains only generic traces such as router, quota, Codex, and account imports. 9Router appears in this article because public OSINT, user descriptions, and the software's documented capabilities closely match the routing layer. It is a relevant example, not proof that every seller in the export runs 9Router.

The current 9Router README publicly describes multi-account support, load balancing, failover when one source hits quota, real-time limit tracking, and combinations that mix subscription, cheap, and free tiers. Its architecture document puts Codex CLI and other clients on one side, the 9Router process in the middle, and OAuth providers, API-key providers, or compatible nodes upstream. That is the shape of a gateway.

Those capabilities have legitimate uses. An individual may balance accounts they own. A team may manage several providers it is authorized to use. A developer may need fallback when a primary service fails. Software does not become illegal merely because it implements round-robin routing.

But the economics change when a gateway is combined with credentials purchased in batches and endpoints sold to unrelated customers. The operator no longer needs to deliver a full Plus account to every buyer. Quota from account A can serve one person, account B another, and both can fail over to account C when limits arrive. A stock of a few hundred accounts can support more sales than accounts because the product is no longer the account. It is access through an intermediary.

Saying that 9Router “creates cheap ChatGPT Plus” assigns it the wrong role. It is closer to an inventory dispatcher than a factory. It does not manufacture the goods, but it helps combine inconsistent stock, consume remaining quota, and keep the storefront more stable than any single account. This amplification lets a small upstream opening serve a much larger downstream market.

The cost equation rules out buying retail and reselling at a loss

There is no sensible business model where a seller buys thousands of Plus subscriptions at list price, resells them for roughly $0.60 to $6, and consistently makes money. If the observed prices are real, at least one assumption in “every customer receives a complete, private, fully paid Plus account” must be false.

Four explanations can coexist. The input cost may genuinely be low because of trials, promotions, regions, activations, or wholesale sources. One account may be sold several times. Quota from many accounts may be pooled and divided among customers. Or the item may simply not be equivalent to a complete private Plus plan. A fifth branch involves money or credentials that do not belong to the seller, but that conclusion requires separate payment or account-takeover evidence.

net profit
= revenue from credentials, seats, quota or relay access
- upstream acquisition cost
- mailboxes, phones, SMS and payment routes
- servers, proxies and pool operations
- dead accounts, replacements, refunds and chargebacks
- reseller, referral and advertising costs

One public self-report placed input cost around 5 RMB per account. Another self-report showed eight survivors from more than 30 accounts. Combining the two only as a sensitivity check changes the effective cost of a live account.

survival rate = 8 / 30 ≈ 26.7%
effective cost = 5 RMB / 26.7% ≈ 18.75 RMB per surviving account

This calculation does not prove anyone's margin. It shows why a cheap product can exist only with aggressive turnover. The operator has to consume quota before the account dies, sell access more than once, use a pool, buy below 5 RMB, or push attrition onto customers through extremely short warranties. The three-hour warranty stops being a strange footnote. It becomes a term in the equation.

Thousands of accounts can be both true and misleading

The export proves batches of dozens to hundreds and contains self-reported claims around 800 to 1,000. Public discussions include claims of pools with thousands of Free accounts. These traces support the existence of batch pipelines. They do not prove that one seller holds hundreds of thousands of unique, fully paid, simultaneously live Plus subscriptions.

At least four units are routinely collapsed into one number. Total accounts ever created include dead accounts. Total credentials or keys delivered may include resales. Total requests through a pool are not accounts. Total Telegram listings may be automated reposts. When a seller claims “10,000,” the first question should be 10,000 of what.

Open Measures identified more than five million Telegram posts in its dataset that appeared to advertise or solicit accounts for many platforms between July 2025 and July 2026. About 429,720 mentioned AI platforms. The 15 most active accounts produced nearly 936,000 posts, and many showed signs of automation. That is strong evidence for distribution at scale. It is also a warning that the number of posts is not the number of unique ChatGPT accounts.

A market can look enormous because it turns over quickly. Accounts are overproduced to absorb attrition, pass through three or four reseller layers, receive new credentials, enter a pool, and generate multiple downstream transactions from their quota. The real scale can still be large. It is large like a flowing river, not like a warehouse that can be captured in one photograph.

This market does not require an OpenAI zero-day

The theory that someone breached OpenAI and obtained unlimited Plus accounts is attractive because it puts everything behind one dramatic secret. The data tells a more ordinary story. Sellers discuss mailboxes, phone verification, payment routes, quota, dead accounts, restocks, and replacements. This is the language of abuse by scale and economic arbitrage, not a stable backend exploit.

OpenAI offers trials and promotional subscriptions subject to eligibility. Referral benefits may provide usage credits, rate-limit resets, or temporary usage, but they are not automatically API credits and may not be sold or transferred. Codex limits vary by plan, model, and task complexity. Those three facts alone create multiple kinds of quota with different value and lifetimes.

Okta Threat Intelligence's Free tokens for sale research describes a gray market using fraudulent registrations to capture trials or credits, then routing requests through relay stations and gateways advertised at discounts of 70 to 90 percent. Okta observed the pattern across several AI services. It does not show that every account in my export came from the same source, but it independently confirms that the architecture account factory → credit or quota → pool → cheap endpoint exists.

Some batches may involve stolen cards, chargebacks, phishing, or leaked credentials. The broader account market certainly contains those branches. Without takeover logs, redacted payment history, disputes, or chain-of-custody evidence, however, calling all cheap stock “carded” only replaces uncertainty with an accusation. Logical inference should connect observed traces, not erase the traces still missing.

The buyer risks more than the price of a coffee

A price of one or two dollars makes failure feel harmless. If the account dies, buy another. But the purchase price is the easiest risk to see. When a user signs into credentials retained by a seller, that seller may have known the password and 2FA secret. When the user calls a relay, every prompt and response must pass through the operator's infrastructure. For Codex, a “prompt” may include source code, file paths, error logs, project configuration, and workplace data.

  • An account may have been drained by a previous user while still displaying a Plus label.
  • The buyer may lose access immediately after the warranty expires.
  • Abuse by another pool user may cause the shared upstream to be suspended.
  • The gateway may log requests and responses for debugging, metering, or other purposes.
  • The model behind an endpoint may not match the advertised model name.
  • Credentials, source code, and payment data may cross several unidentified resellers.

OpenAI says an account is intended for the individual who created it and warns that sharing credentials increases privacy and misuse risk. Its current Terms of Use also prohibit sharing credentials, making an account available to others, and circumventing rate limits or protective measures. Terms do not establish the source of a particular batch, but they show that a pool customer relies on access that can be revoked at any time.

An OpenAI-compatible endpoint does not mean the request goes directly to OpenAI. “Compatible” describes an interface shape, not provenance, privacy, or ownership of quota.

The gateway itself becomes an attack surface

A router holds the most valuable parts of the system, including tokens, accounts, API keys, usage logs, and sometimes request content. If its dashboard or proxy is exposed incorrectly, a gateway flaw can turn the account pool into free infrastructure for strangers or make the router host an entry point into the operator's machine.

9Router has had several public advisories, each fixed in particular releases. An authorization bypass affected versions before 0.3.75. A chain involving unauthenticated MCP plugin routes allowed remote command execution from 0.4.30 to versions before 0.4.37. Another advisory described unauthenticated /v1 proxy access and SSRF through 0.4.80, patched in 0.4.82.

Mentioning these flaws does not mean 9Router is always malware or that current versions retain every old issue. It means operators need to patch, require API keys, avoid exposing dashboards casually, restrict networks, and understand what logs are stored. The more accounts are concentrated in one place, the larger the impact of careless configuration. Pooling reduces user friction while concentrating operational risk.

When the platform tightens controls, legitimate users breathe the dust too

A pool creates traffic unlike one individual. It produces multiple sessions, varied IPs, parallel requests, quota consumed near the ceiling, and account replacement as soon as limits arrive. A provider has incentives to increase phone verification, device linking, relationship detection, rate limits, and promotion revocation. The market's “sweeps” may be the visible side of that chase, although the precise cause cannot be assigned without OpenAI logs.

Anti-abuse controls rarely touch only sellers. A student with several devices, a developer changing networks, a traveling team, or a legitimate heavy user may encounter new verification and harsher limits. The gray market profits from gaps in the system, while the cost of controlling it is spread across the wider community.

This does not make official buyers the only victims or absolve OpenAI of responsibility. If plans, quotas, credits, and usage metering are too difficult to understand, ambiguity becomes reseller territory. If a third-party endpoint can present itself as an “official Codex API” without any way for users to verify the model, source, or privacy boundary, the provider also has work to do in product transparency, compromise warnings, and session management.

The boundary is not the number of accounts

A person may legitimately own several accounts. A company may have many seats. A team may build an internal gateway for resources it is authorized to use. 9Router, codex2api, or a load balancer is not inherently culpable because it contains the word pool.

The suspicious boundary appears when several signs converge. Credentials are sold to unrelated people. Stock is replenished continuously after deaths. Replacement warranties become routine. Sources are hidden behind endpoints. Referrals, trials, or seats are commercialized against their intended purpose. Buyers do not know where prompts travel. Sellers cannot or will not explain their upstream rights.

This distinction matters because a useful investigation should not flatten every tool into “hacking.” Doing so harms legitimate use cases and gives genuinely questionable operators an easy defense that the software is merely open source. A neutral tool does not remove responsibility for deployment. A questionable deployment does not make every user of the tool a criminal.

If OpenAI later documents additional trial mechanics, changes UPI, ends a promotion, revises Codex metering, or shuts down one pipeline, this article does not instantly become a joke. It is a dated snapshot of a fast-changing market. The source openings, survival rates, and prices would need updates. The broader economic architecture would be falsified only by evidence that the listings deliver no real access, the pools do not use upstream accounts as described, or the export's restock and replacement chain was fabricated.

Conversely, if redacted payment data later proves chargebacks in one batch, account-takeover logs emerge, or a technical response confirms an exploit, the conclusion should become stronger. Leaving room for new evidence is not withdrawing the argument. It is how an investigation avoids freezing today's inference into permanent truth.

Questions to ask before accepting a bargain

Not everyone buying a cheap account understands that they are entering a reseller chain. Some only need Codex for coursework or a personal project and cannot justify the official price. An affordable price, however, does not erase questions about privacy, ownership, and data risk.

  • Is this a private account, seat, activation, or relay endpoint?
  • Who controls the email, recovery methods, 2FA, and existing sessions?
  • Are prompts and responses logged, where are logs stored, and for how long?
  • How are the model and quota verified beyond the seller's claim?
  • When an account dies, is it replaced or refunded, and what happens to old data?
  • Does the seller have the right to provide that seat or entitlement?

For source code, tokens, client documents, or workplace data, the safest answer remains not to send them through an uncontrolled relay. If purchased credentials have already been used, avoid personal data, do not attach your own payment method, and never reuse passwords. If you operate a legitimate gateway, apply patches, restrict network access, require authentication, inspect logging, and keep secrets away from public dashboards.

Sources and how they should be read

The Account Sharing Policy, Terms of Use, multi-currency billing documentation, Codex usage limits, Codex Referral Promotions, and the trial FAQ establish normal behavior, official limits, and benefits. They are not used to dismiss real-world traces merely because OpenAI has not published an article confirming each abuse pattern.

The 9Router repository and architecture documentation are primary sources for multi-account support, fallback, quota tracking, and gateway behavior. The authorization bypass, MCP plugin RCE, and open relay and SSRF advisories describe risks in affected versions. They do not show that 9Router creates accounts or that every release is malicious.

Okta Threat Intelligence independently supports the model of abused trials or credits sold through relays. Open Measures provides context for account trafficking and automated distribution on Telegram. LINUX DO, Reddit, VOZ, and marketplace listings are treated as market traces and self-reports, not audited statistics.

The private export is the direct source for menus, prices, inventory, warranties, and seller language. Because it contains logins, 2FA data, and sensitive screenshots, it is not attached here. The original is preserved with the hash stated above. Withholding credentials makes individual messages harder for readers to reproduce, but publishing them would turn an investigation into a new breach. That limitation is intentional.

This market does not sell ChatGPT, it sells ambiguity

The most profitable asset may not be the account. It may be the distance between the label a buyer understands and the asset actually delivered. “Plus” may be a trial. “API” may be a relay. “One month” may only be an advertised duration attached to an account that lives three days. A “warranty” may mean the right to receive another credential from the same unstable batch. “Unlimited” may mean the router keeps changing sources until the whole pool is empty.

9Router makes that distance easier to operate, but it does not create it. Low-cost supply comes from a mixture of promotions, payment routes, account factories, seats, idle quota, layered resale, and possibly illicit sources. The router makes inconsistent resources behind the scenes look like one continuous product in front.

The sound criticism is therefore not “every cheap account was hacked” or “9Router is a virus.” It is that a market is selling AI access while deliberately obscuring ownership, lifespan, model identity, quota source, and the path taken by user data. When the system fails, the customer absorbs the dead account. When controls tighten, legitimate users absorb more verification. When the gateway leaks, prompts and credentials may become the prize. The seller keeps the spread while uncertainty is distributed to everyone else.

You made it to the end The short version
Is a $1.50 ChatGPT Plus account an official full Plus plan?

That cannot be assumed. The same label may describe a short-lived account, trial, promotion, activation, seat, resold credentials, or quota served through a pool.

Does 9Router create cheap accounts?

There is no evidence that it creates accounts. It is a gateway that can combine accounts, track quota, balance requests, and switch sources when one account hits a limit or fails.

Are cheap accounts necessarily hacked or paid with stolen cards?

No. Those sources may exist, but price alone does not prove them. Trials, promotions, regional routes, seats, account factories, pools, and layered resale can also lower prices.

What is the largest relay risk?

The user must trust an intermediary with prompts, source code, credentials, and traffic. They also inherit account attrition, depleted quota, model substitution, other users' behavior, and gateway flaws.
